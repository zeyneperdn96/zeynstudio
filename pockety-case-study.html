Problem

Budgeting shouldn't feel like accounting

Most budgeting apps feel overwhelming, data-heavy, or visually stressful. They present too much information at once, use intimidating financial jargon, and treat every user like a finance professional. The result: users often abandon them after just a few days.

Young professionals and students want to control their daily spending but dislike complex finance apps. They need clarity, not complexity.

Solution

Simplicity as a design strategy

Pockety focuses on reducing cognitive load. Every screen serves a single purpose, and the interface guides users through financial tracking without making them think about finance.

One-tap expense entry

Adding an expense takes three taps: amount, category, done. No friction, no extra fields, no overthinking.

Smart categorization

Pre-built categories with clear icons reduce decision fatigue. Users can also create custom categories that fit their lifestyle.

Visual spending insights

Charts and breakdowns tell the story of spending habits at a glance. No spreadsheets, no complex graphs, just clarity.

Gentle budget alerts

Reminders are informative, not stressful. When approaching a budget limit, users get a friendly nudge instead of a warning.

UX Decisions

Design decisions that matter

Every interaction was shaped by one principle: reduce anxiety around money. Here are the key decisions and the reasoning behind them.

Large tap targets for quick entry

The expense input field and category cards are oversized intentionally. Users logging expenses are often in a hurry, so every tap target needs to be easy to hit without precision.

Minimal visual noise

Money already causes stress. The interface uses generous whitespace, neutral backgrounds, and soft shadows to create a calm environment where numbers feel manageable.

Clear hierarchy for balance and spending

The most important number, total balance, is always the largest element on screen. Secondary information like monthly spend and remaining budget follows in a clear visual order.

Informative alerts, not stressful ones

Budget limit notifications use friendly language and offer a clear next step ("View Overview") instead of just showing a warning. The "Not Now" option respects the user's pace.

Friendly empty states

First-time users see encouraging prompts instead of blank screens. Empty states are designed to guide the first action rather than leaving users wondering what to do next.

Blue accent for trust

The primary blue was chosen deliberately. In fintech, blue conveys reliability and security. Combined with rounded cards and modern typography, it creates a professional yet approachable feel.

Final Thoughts

What I learned

Designing a finance app taught me that the hardest part isn't showing data, it's deciding what not to show. Here are the key takeaways.

Less data, more understanding

Early versions showed too many metrics at once. Reducing the dashboard to balance, weekly chart, and recent transactions made the information actually useful instead of just available.

Emotional design in fintech

Finance apps carry emotional weight. Using soft colors, rounded shapes, and friendly language transforms what could feel stressful into something approachable. The visual tone directly affects how users feel about their finances.

Speed builds habits

The 10-second expense logging goal was the most impactful design constraint. When an action is fast enough, users do it consistently. When it takes too long, they abandon it.

Constraints drive better design

Limiting the scope to expense tracking, budgets, and insights forced sharper design decisions. Every feature had to earn its place on screen. The result is a more focused and usable product.
